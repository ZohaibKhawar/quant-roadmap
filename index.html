How to become a quant

Six stages through the maths, code, markets and interviews, with a problem at each one so you can tell whether you're ready to move on.

Three jobs share the title

"Quant" means someone who uses maths and code to make decisions in markets. The day-to-day work is very different depending on the seat. Pick one and the roadmap will mark what's core for it, or leave it on all three if you aren't sure yet.

Quant researcher Quant trader Quant developer
The work Finds patterns in data that predict prices, then tests whether they survive costs and fresh data. Runs and adjusts strategies live, prices risk and makes fast calls when markets move. Builds the trading systems, data pipelines and research tools everything else runs on.
You'd like it if You can stay with one hard question for months, knowing most ideas will fail. You enjoy games, quick estimates and being accountable for results every day. You care about why code is slow and enjoy making systems reliable.
Usual education Often a master's or PhD in a maths-heavy field. The most credential-heavy of the three. Bachelor's or master's in a quantitative field. Many firms hire straight from undergrad. Computer science or similar at bachelor's or master's level. A PhD is rarely needed.
Interviews test Probability, statistics, machine learning, coding and your past research. Probability, mental maths, betting and market-making games. Algorithms, C++ or another core language, operating systems and system design.

There are other doors, too. Risk and model validation teams at banks, derivatives pricing ("strats") desks, data engineering at a fund and systematic asset managers all use the same skills. Many of them hire more widely, and some people move from them to the seats above.

The roadmap

Do the stages roughly in order, though stages 1 to 4 overlap in practice. Tick things off as you go; your progress stays in this browser. Time estimates are rough and assume steady part-time study alongside school or work.

1

Maths foundations

About 3 to 6 months. Skip what your degree already covered.

Everything later is written in this language. You don't need to be a mathematician, but you do need to be comfortable reading a formula and working out what it's saying.

Where to learn it

  • MIT 18.06 Linear Algebra, Gilbert Strang's free lecture course
  • Essence of Linear Algebra, free videos that build the geometric picture
  • MIT 18.02SC Multivariable Calculus, a free self-study course
  • Book of Proof by Richard Hammack, free online
  • Zetamac, the arithmetic drill interview candidates use
Ready to move on? Two assets have the covariance matrix [2 1; 1 2]. Find the eigenvalues. Which unit-length portfolio has the lowest variance?

Solve (2 − λ)² − 1 = 0, which gives λ = 3 and λ = 1.

The lowest-variance direction is the eigenvector for λ = 1: (1, −1)/√2, long one asset and short the other, with variance 1. The direction (1, 1)/√2 holds both and has variance 3. The two assets move together, so taking opposite sides cancels the movement they share.

2

Probability and statistics

About 4 to 8 months. Give this stage the most time.

This is the core of the job and of most interviews. Learn it by solving a lot of problems, since watching lectures alone won't make it stick.

Where to learn it

  • Harvard Stat 110, Joe Blitzstein's free probability course, with lectures and problems
  • Introduction to Probability by Blitzstein and Hwang, the course's book, free online
  • MIT 18.650 Statistics for Applications, free lectures
  • Seeing Theory, free visual explanations for building intuition
  • Fifty Challenging Problems in Probability by Frederick Mosteller, a short, cheap classic
  • All of Statistics by Larry Wasserman, a fast tour of statistics if you already know the maths
Ready to move on? You flip a fair coin until you get two heads in a row. How many flips do you expect? Is the answer the same for heads then tails?

Let E be the expected number of flips. Split on how the start goes: tails first (probability ½) wastes 1 flip, heads then tails (¼) wastes 2, and heads then heads (¼) finishes in 2.

E = ½(1 + E) + ¼(2 + E) + ¼(2), which gives E = 6.

Heads then tails takes only 4 on average. Once you've thrown a head, a miss on HT is another head, which keeps your progress. A miss on HH throws it all away.

And one more: You test 20 strategies that have no real edge, at the 5% significance level. What's the chance at least one looks significant?

If the tests are independent, 1 − 0.95²⁰ ≈ 64%. So finding a "significant" strategy after trying many says very little on its own. Firms are strict about this because it's how most false discoveries happen.

3

Programming

About 3 to 6 months for the basics, then keep going throughout.

Everyone needs Python. C++ is where the routes split: it's essential for most developer jobs and optional for many researchers and traders.

Where to learn it

  • Python for Data Analysis by Wes McKinney, the creator of pandas, free online
  • Learn C++, a free, thorough tutorial
  • The Missing Semester, MIT's free course on the shell, Git and other tools
  • NeetCode, an organised list of coding interview problems
  • Computer Systems: A Programmer's Perspective by Bryant and O'Hallaron, for how machines really run your code
  • Effective Modern C++ by Scott Meyers, once you know the basics
Ready to move on? Estimate π with a million random points and no Python loop. How much more data do you need to make the error 10 times smaller? 
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng()
n = 1_000_000
x, y = rng.random(n), rng.random(n)
print(4 * np.mean(x**2 + y**2 < 1))

The fraction of points inside the quarter circle estimates π/4. The standard error is about 0.0016 at this size and shrinks with 1/√n, so 10 times the accuracy takes 100 times the samples.

4

Markets and models

About 3 to 6 months. Firms will teach you plenty of this on the job.

Many firms care more about how you think than what you already know about finance. You still need enough to speak the language, and to understand why noisy market data breaks methods that work elsewhere.

Where to learn it

  • MIT 18.S096 Mathematics with Applications in Finance, free lectures, some given by people from industry
  • An Introduction to Statistical Learning, a free book with Python labs
  • Options, Futures, and Other Derivatives by John Hull, the standard reference
  • Trading and Exchanges by Larry Harris, on how markets are built
  • Active Portfolio Management by Grinold and Kahn, on how systematic portfolios are run
  • Stochastic Calculus for Finance by Steven Shreve, if you're heading toward derivatives
Ready to move on? A stock trades at 100. A one-year European call with strike 100 costs 10, and the continuously compounded rate is 5%. What should the put cost? If it trades at 6, how do you profit?

Put-call parity says C − P = S − Ke−rT, so P = 10 − 100 + 100e−0.05 ≈ 5.12.

At 6, the put is too expensive. Sell the put (+6), buy the call (−10), short the stock (+100) and lend 95.12 at 5% (−95.12). You collect about 0.88 today. In one year the loan pays back 100, which is exactly what you need: wherever the stock ends up, the call and put together let you buy a share at 100 and close the short, leaving nothing owed. This ignores costs and borrowing fees, which is why gaps like this rarely last in real markets.

5

Proof of work

About 3 to 6 months, alongside the earlier stages.

This is your evidence that you can do the work, and it matters most if your school or degree won't open doors by itself. A few honest, well-tested projects beat a long list of tutorials. An impressive-looking return with no explanation will count against you.

Where to find it

  • IMC Prosperity, a trading contest run by the firm IMC
  • Kaggle competitions, including some past market prediction contests
  • Figgie, a card game Jane Street created to teach trading
  • Jane Street puzzles, a new one each month
  • Kenneth French Data Library, free factor return data, good for reproducing studies
  • FRED, free economic data from the St. Louis Fed
Ready to move on? Your backtest shows a Sharpe ratio of 3 over ten years. Name four reasons it might be fake, and how you'd check each one.
  • Look-ahead bias. The strategy used data it wouldn't have had yet. Shift every input by one bar and see if the result collapses.
  • Survivorship bias. The data leaves out companies that went bankrupt or were delisted. Test on a point-in-time universe that includes them.
  • Unrealistic costs. It ignores spreads, fees and the market impact of your own trades. Rerun with realistic costs, then double them.
  • Overfitting. You tried many versions and kept the best. Count your trials, and test once on data you've never touched.

6

Getting hired

About 2 to 4 months of focused prep before applications open.

The usual way in is an internship, which often turns into a full-time offer. Prepare early, because the best-known programs fill long before their summer starts.

When to apply

Dates vary by firm and keep moving earlier, so check each careers page from the spring. For a summer internship, a recent cycle looked roughly like this:

  1. Spring, the year before Some top firms open applications. Finish your resume.
  2. July and August Most remaining firms have opened. Apply straight away, since many review applications as they come in.
  3. Summer to autumn Online tests, phone screens and final rounds.
  4. October to November Many trading firms make their offers.
  5. Winter Hedge funds and smaller firms are still hiring. Keep applying.

Where to practise

  • A Practical Guide to Quantitative Finance Interviews by Xinfeng Zhou, known as the "green book"
  • Heard on the Street by Timothy Crack, a long-running question collection
  • Quant Job Interview Questions and Answers by Joshi, Denson and Downes, mainly for derivatives roles
  • OpenQuant and QuantGuide, online question banks
  • Brainstellar, free puzzles sorted by difficulty
Ready to apply? "Make me a market on the sum of two dice." Then: "The first die is a 6. Where's your market now?"

The sum averages 7 with a standard deviation of about 2.4, so a quote such as 6.5 bid, 7.5 offered is sensible: you'll buy at 6.5 or sell at 7.5, and earn the spread if orders come from both sides.

Given a 6, the sum runs from 7 to 12 and averages 9.5, with a standard deviation of about 1.7. Move your quote to around 9 bid, 10 offered. The interviewer is watching how quickly you update, and how you react when someone keeps trading against you. That's a hint they may know something you don't, so widen your quote and move it.

What to know before you commit

If you're not at a well-known school

School name matters, especially for research roles, and top firms get far more applicants than they have seats. It isn't the only way through.

  • Apply anyway. Trading and developer roles often rely on online tests and interviews, so strong candidates can get noticed by doing well on them.
  • Earn a signal. Contest placements and rigorous public projects are hard to fake and don't depend on your school.
  • Consider a master's. Programs such as Baruch's MFE, Carnegie Mellon's MSCF and Toronto's MMF recruit heavily into the industry, but they're expensive and competitive. Compare them on the QuantNet rankings, and look closely at where graduates end up.
  • Enter through a nearby door. Software jobs, or risk and model validation at a bank, build relevant experience and pay well in their own right.

Pay and the trade-offs

Reported first-year pay at top US firms is very high, roughly US$200,000 to US$450,000 including bonus, with some firms above that. Many of these figures are self-reported or estimated, and they mostly describe a small number of elite firms. Banks, smaller firms and jobs outside the main US hubs usually pay less.

A large share of that pay is often a bonus tied to results, and teams can be cut when they stop making money. Hours are often long, and many ideas fail. Contracts may also include non-competes that limit where you can go next.

Common mistakes

  • Starting with day trading or a trading bot before you can test one honestly. Live profits over a few months prove little, and losing real money teaches you less than a careful study would.
  • Paying for expensive bootcamps or certificates. Firms hire on interview performance and evidence of your skills.
  • Collecting courses instead of solving problems. If you can't solve the practice problems, watching another lecture won't fix it.
  • Only applying to the five most famous firms, then stopping when they say no.
